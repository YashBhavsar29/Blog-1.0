Chasing Perfection: Why I Know Better and Still Can’t Stop

On doing things with intention, getting stuck anyway, and what I’m learning along the way.

Here’s a small confession from someone who tracks his expenses, his screen time and his schedule: I know that chasing perfection is not always a good idea. I’ve read the books. I can explain the theory. I could probably give a short talk on it. ⚡

And yet, when I sit down to do something, I still catch myself spending far too long trying to do it perfectly.

It takes time. It drains my energy. Sometimes it leaves me mentally stuck, staring at one small detail while the rest of the work waits. And even after all that, letting go is hard.

I want to write about this honestly, because I think many of you know this feeling, even if you call it something else.

THE CONFESSIONI know. I still do it. 🔁

Knowing something and doing it are very different things, and perfectionism is the best example I have.

You rewrite one sentence five times. You fix the alignment of something nobody else will ever notice. You delay starting because the plan isn’t perfect yet. The work isn’t finished, but it feels like work, so it feels okay. ⌚

The funny part is that I’m often trying to save time. I plan carefully, I organise, I want to avoid mistakes so I won’t have to redo things later. But perfectionism quietly takes back every hour I was trying to save.

THE REAL ISSUEIt’s not really about being flawless

Here’s something I had to figure out slowly: for me, this isn’t about wanting to be flawless, or wanting everyone to clap.

It’s the deep urge to do things with intention. With care. With precision. I want my work to feel like it was made by someone who meant it, and that’s a good thing. Caring about quality is not the problem.

The problem starts when “doing it with care” quietly becomes “I can’t stop until it’s perfect.” Those two feel almost the same from the inside, but they lead to very different places.

Care says: “Let me do this well.”
Perfectionism says: “Let me not do this until I can’t fail.” — note to self

The first one moves you forward. The second one keeps you standing still.

THE STORYThe portfolio that never got built 🕹️

The best example I have of this is my portfolio.

For almost a quarter of a year, I was going to build the perfect one. Notice the wording: not building, going to build.

It started with something beautiful. I’d come across portfolios like Bruno Simon’s, and a few others, which aren’t websites in the usual sense. They’re small 3D worlds built with Three.js. You move around inside them, play with the animations, and while you play, you slowly find out who the person is, what they’ve made and what they care about. It felt like a game that happened to be a resume.

I was hooked. That’s the one I want.

So I planned everything. Then, one by one, the obstacles showed up:

  • To make the 3D models, I had to learn Blender.
  • To bring them to life, I had to get much better at Three.js.
  • To run Blender properly, I needed a powerful PC, and I don’t have one.
  • And all of it needed a lot, lot of time.

I treated each of these as a gate I had to pass before I could start. So I spent days figuring out how to build 3D models, watching tutorials, researching and “preparing”. I felt busy. I felt serious. Planning feels like progress, which is exactly why it’s so dangerous.

planning feels like progress. it isn’t.

Meanwhile, I had a bigger roadmap in my head, and of course it was perfect:

  1. Build a cool portfolio.
  2. Make a resume.
  3. Hunt for internships.
  4. Be working, and getting paid, within a month or two.

Then life got in the way. Hackathons. SIH (the Smart India Hackathon, a big national contest). Tests. Submissions. Regular classes. Home visits during vacations. Festivals. Every one of them was worth doing, and every one of them pushed my plan a little further away.

When I finally finished everything else and sat down to get back to the portfolio, I noticed something uncomfortable. Nothing was built. No pages, no models, nothing. After all that time, what I had was a plan.

I’d been busy designing the perfect portfolio, and I hadn’t made one.

THE TURNThe thing I learned the hard way 🎯

Around then I watched a lot of videos about how a portfolio should look and how to build one. And one thing kept showing up.

I’m applying for game programming roles. A recruiter looking at someone like me isn’t going to care how magical my website’s 3D world is. Honestly, they’d barely notice it. I looked at the other people in my space who were applying, and most of them had pretty normal portfolios. What set them apart was what was inside: the projects, and the thinking behind them.

The dream version of my portfolio was built for me, and not for the people who’d be reading it.

I won’t pretend that was easy to accept. Dropping the idea took a lot of courage. I’d spent so long imagining it that letting go felt like admitting the time was wasted. But it was the first useful thing I’d done in months.

So I started over with a normal portfolio. It’s not the best or the nicest one out there. But it’s the one recruiters will actually see, and the one that shows what matters.

I built it in less than a week.

Think about that for a second: almost a quarter of a year for a portfolio that never existed, and less than a week for one that does. 🤯

The perfect portfolio never went live. The normal one did.

THE COSTWhat it costs me ⚖️

Let me be honest about the price of this habit, with that story fresh in mind:

  • Time. In my case, a full quarter of a year, and a lot of small hours before that.
  • Energy. I finish tired, even when I haven’t done much new work.
  • Starting. Sometimes I don’t begin at all, because the first step won’t be perfect.
  • Peace. Even when something is good, I’m already thinking about what’s wrong with it.

And the strangest part is that perfectionism can make me feel productive while keeping me stuck. Planning and polishing are easier than finishing, because finishing means somebody might see it.

THE SHIFTSo I try to shift my focus 🧭

Different experiences on this journey have taught me that chasing perfection alone is exhausting. So I’m trying something else.

Instead of asking, “Is this perfect?” I ask, “Does this reflect me?”

I put more effort into building something that shows my thoughts, my values and my journey. My normal portfolio does that far better than my dream one ever could, because it’s mine and it’s real. It’s honest. That’s a much better thing to chase, because I can actually reach it.

THE DIFFERENCERespect is slower than recognition 🤝

I’ve also noticed something about people. Recognition is quick. A flashy design, a good result, a good post, and people say “wow.” But it fades just as fast.

Respect is different. It’s earned slowly, and it comes from doing things with honesty and discipline, again and again, even when nobody is watching. A 3D world might get a “wow” once. Clear projects and honest thinking get trust for years.

I’d rather earn that.

THE CYCLELearning, unlearning, and starting again 🧠

Along the way I’ve had to unlearn a lot: old habits, limiting beliefs and even outdated ways of thinking that I carried without noticing. “A portfolio must be amazing” was one of them.

I used to think learning was a one-time thing: you learn something and it’s yours forever. But it’s more like a cycle:

  1. Pick up a new idea.
  2. Let go of what you no longer need.
  3. Start again, with an open mind.

It’s tough, because letting go of an old belief feels like losing a part of yourself. But it’s freeing too. Every time I drop a rule that wasn’t helping me, I find I have more room to breathe.

THREE LESSONSThree things I learned along the way 📌

These are the lessons I keep coming back to. I think they’ll be useful to you too.

LESSON 1Pay attention to what truly matters 🎯

There’s always a lot going on around us: distractions, other people’s opinions, things pulling us in different directions. It’s easy to spend a whole day busy and still feel like nothing important got done.

That’s why it’s so important to decide what truly matters to us. We don’t have endless time or energy, so we should spend both on the things that actually help us grow or bring us peace.

My portfolio is a clear example. What mattered was the projects and the thinking behind them. I was spending my energy on 3D models. Perfectionism spreads our care evenly over everything. Focus puts it where it counts.

LESSON 2Small steps, every single day 🔮

Getting better doesn’t always mean doing something big. Even small improvements, done every day, can make a big difference over time. It might not show up right away, but it adds up, slowly and surely.

The key is to keep going, even on days when we don’t feel like it.

What shapes our future isn’t only how talented we are. It’s how patient and consistent we stay, even when it’s tough. A perfect day happens once in a while. An okay day, repeated a hundred times, builds a life. And a simple site built in a week beats a perfect one that was never started.

LESSON 3Push past the limits you didn’t know you set 🏆

We set limits for ourselves, sometimes without even realising it. “I’m not good at this.” “That’s for other people.” “Maybe later.”

We should try to push past those limits. Go after goals that feel a bit scary or out of reach. That’s usually where real growth begins: in the moments we step out of our comfort zone and try something that challenges us.

You might think this clashes with everything I said about the portfolio, but I don’t think it does. Dreaming big was never the mistake. The mistake was turning the dream into a condition for starting. A big goal is fine. Waiting for perfect conditions before you take the first step is what stops us. Perfection says “don’t try until you can’t fail.” Growth says “try, and learn from the failing.” I’m choosing the second.

LAST WORDKeep the spark. Stay humble. 🏁

As we move forward, I think we should keep that spark alive: always wanting to learn more, try new things and grow.

At the same time, we need to stay humble and remind ourselves that we don’t know everything yet, and that’s okay.

It’s easy to get comfortable. It’s easy to stop aiming higher once things are going fine. But deep down, we know we can do more and be more.

Takeaway

A normal portfolio built in a week beats a perfect one that never exists. So don’t settle for less than what you’re truly capable of, and don’t wait until it’s perfect to begin. 🏁

— Yash
