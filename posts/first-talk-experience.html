“Bhaiya, Are You Yash Bhavsar?”: My First Talk to My Juniors

A stranger in the hostel, a long night of thinking, and a talk that took me out of my comfort zone.

About a year ago, in my final year of diploma, I stood in front of a class of first-year students and gave a talk. 🎙

I wrote a short post about it on LinkedIn back then, while the feeling was still fresh. But I wanted the full story to live here, in my notebook, so here it is.

One more thing before we start: I gave this talk in Marathi, our own language, so that everyone in the room could follow it easily. In this post I’ve translated everything into English, so it’s understandable to everyone. 🏫

THE INVITATIONA question in the hostel 😅

It began on an ordinary day, I was visiting in the hostel.

A group of juniors was passing by. I didn’t know any of them. Then one of them stopped, turned and asked:

“Bhaiya, are you Yash Bhavsar?”

I said yes, a bit confused. He told me that he and some of his classmates had seen my profile on LinkedIn, and they’d even shown it to their teacher. They had liked it so much that they wanted to invite me to their class, to give some guidance to the first-year students.

I didn’t know what to say. Nobody had ever asked me anything like that. It took me a moment just to digest the question, let alone the request. So I did the only thing I could: I told him I’d let him know in a day or two, and we exchanged contacts.

His name was Arjun.

THE DECISIONThe night I couldn’t stop thinking 🌙

Back in my room, I couldn’t think about anything else. I talked it over with Anand, my best friend and the same friend who’d ridden with me across the city looking for an internship.

The more I thought about it, the clearer it became: this was exactly the kind of opportunity I’d always wanted.

I’ve always been inspired by people who give talks: TED talks, famous speeches, all of it. I’d given a few speeches myself back in my school days, but they were for events like Teachers’ Day and Independence Day. In every one of them, I told the stories of people from the past who had won in life.

I had never told my own story.

I write my stories in LinkedIn posts and in my journal, but standing on a stage and saying them out loud was something I’d honestly love to do. And that’s where the real challenge sat. I’m an introvert, someone who fears speaking even a word in a crowd. Developing the courage to go there was the hard part, much more than the talk itself.

But I decided. I called Arjun and said:

“Yes. I’ll be there next week.”

THE SCRIPTWhat do I even say? 📝

Once I knew I’d do it, my head filled up with questions:

  • What could I tell them?
  • How should I start?
  • What’s actually useful for a first-year student?
  • And what if I say something wrong?

I thought about it a lot. It was really hard for me, because I overthink everything, and this was a talk about my own story, which made it feel even more personal.

I was also deep in practicals and other work at the time, so free time was tight. Still, I finalised the date, and then I did what I always do when I’m unsure: I wrote everything down. I prepared a script for the talk.

THE NERVESThe nerves before the door

Even with the script, I was nervous before I went. What if I miss something? What if I forget an important point? What if nobody reacts?

It’s a strange feeling. You know the topic because it’s your own journey, and still your heart doesn’t believe you.

THE MOMENTThen I walked in 😬

THE MOMENT

I started a little scared. I picked up a piece of chalk and wrote a few words on the board, in my worst handwriting, so messy that even I couldn’t understand it.

Then I turned and looked at the whole class, and I went blank. I just started speaking the words I had crammed, in a brittle, under-confident voice.

The teacher noticed, and told me to relax.

I took a deep breath, closed my eyes for a split second, and told myself:

Yash, you can do it. — me, to me, with my eyes closed
worst handwriting. even I couldn’t read it.

And then I faced it.

THE RELIEFAnd then it went really well ✔

Everything after that went good. I asked them a few questions, which were part of my script. They asked a few doubts too, and I was able to solve them. The students were interested, they stayed engaged, and by the end it was a really nice, interactive session. 🎓

It wasn’t a lecture, it was a conversation. And it was much better than the version my nerves had imagined.

THE TALKWhat I told them

Here’s what I actually shared, translated from the script. My topic was online courses. But I didn’t want to talk about which courses to take. I wanted to talk about why we should do courses at all, and why online learning matters.

THE TALK · OPENERI started with a question

Before the topic, I asked them something:

You all came to diploma after your 10th. Some of you came just because your parents said so. Some of you came with a dream. It could be anything: a web developer, a software developer. Can two or three of you tell me what your dream is, and why you chose diploma?

A few students shared theirs. Mostly the kinds of dreams I expected: data scientist, web developer, and so on.

THE TALK · MY REASONA lockdown, a game and a YouTube search 🎮

Then I told them about my first day in first year. In the very first lecture, our English madam asked everyone to introduce themselves and say what their dream was. I said I wanted to be a game developer.

I told them that behind every dream there should be a reason. The reason can be anything, but it has to be there. If you want to be a web developer only because your friend is becoming one, or because the job pays well, it will be very hard to survive in this field.

So I told them my reason.

During the lockdown, we were all at home with no school and no studies. Like many others, I was just passing time, mostly playing Free Fire. Months went by like that. But as my 10th boards came closer, something inside me said, this has to stop.

So I asked myself a different question: what do I actually like? And I realised I was curious about how games are made. I started searching on YouTube. That’s how I learned that programming languages exist, and that one of them, C#, can be used to make games. That’s also when I understood that “game developer” is an actual career path.

I was honest with them about what happened next. I learned some C# before my final boards, but there was a gap of 3 to 4 months and I forgot most of it. The language was also tricky, and I couldn’t find a good tutorial, so my programming basics never became clear.

After my 10th, during the vacation, I kept exploring. That’s when I found CodeWithHarry’s Python course and followed it. From there, my interest in programming kept growing. By the time I took admission to diploma, I had already learned Python, C, C++ and Data Structures, only by watching those tutorials.

THE TALK · THE GAPWhy college alone isn’t enough

I told them that everything I’d learned before diploma is something they would also learn in their three years of diploma, and later in degree.

But college teaching is mostly at the theory level. We study subjects for the paper, and once the final exam is over, we forget most of it.

And when we go into the industry, our skills matter far more than our degree or diploma certificate. You can’t learn how real software is built by writing code on a notebook page.

That’s why I told them to focus on online learning alongside college.

THE TALK · CLASSESAbout paid classes

Many students join classes: M1, M2, C, Java and many more. But I often hear that even in those classes, the teaching is mostly theoretical.

I told them that personally, I never joined classes during my diploma, because I never needed to. Most of what classes charge for is available online for free, on YouTube and on many other platforms.

I wasn’t saying “don’t join classes.” That’s your choice. I only asked them to think about this: if you used that same time on an online course, how much could you gain?

THE TALK · FOUR RULESFour things that improve your skills

Of course, following online courses doesn’t mean following every course you find. I told them four things matter.

RULE 1Explore your curiosity

You have two to three years. It feels like a lot, but it passes quickly. If you try to explore everything, time will run out and you’ll learn nothing completely.

So learn what makes you curious. In my case, I wanted to be a game developer, but I was also interested in Android and web development, so I explored those too. Some of my friends are interested in cyber security and IoT hardware, so they explore those.

You could even plan it semester by semester: web development this semester, data science the next. By the end, you’d have explored three or four fields.

Some friends tell me I’m giving the wrong advice, that you should learn only the field you want a career in. But my view is that in industry, whether you’re applying for a job or starting your own startup, it’s expected that you know a little about every field.

RULE 2Practice by doing

Say you’re watching a four-hour tutorial series. Do you watch all four hours in one day? It’s not a movie. Watching it all at once doesn’t mean you’ve learned it.

You need real practice. Run what the video teaches on your own computer. Change something and see how the output changes.

coding = problem solving.

Many people think coding is just writing programs, but coding is really problem solving: how you think through a problem, and how you turn those steps into a program. Programming languages are mostly similar, and only the syntax, the way of writing, changes. Once your problem-solving mindset develops, you can write code in any language. Picking up a language can take about a day. Learning to solve problems takes much longer, and the more you practise, the faster you learn.

RULE 3Build projects

Writing small programs is good practice, but it doesn’t mean you now know coding. You also need to understand how a small piece of code works inside a large system, and what happens to a whole website or app if there’s a small mistake in it. You only learn that by building projects.

You don’t have to build everything on your own at the start. If you don’t know how to build a project, follow an online tutorial and code along with it. You’ll still learn a lot, and later, you’ll get ideas for projects of your own.

And the projects you build yourself are worth a lot in industry. In interviews, they ask what projects you’ve made and how you made them. If you built them yourself, you have a clear advantage.

RULE 4Learn up-to-date technologies

The last point: stay updated with new technologies. A lot is going on around AI right now. There are AI tools that can build an entire website without you writing any code, so you should at least know such tools exist.

You’ll also see ads saying “Become an AI Expert for ₹99” or “Become an AI Master.” Don’t fall for them. Professors from places like Harvard and Stanford, who know far more than the people selling those courses, have their courses available for free on YouTube and other platforms.

And one more thing: when you learn technologies, first learn Git and GitHub. It’s a version control system. It lets you maintain versions of your software, and it records who changed what in the code, and when.

THE TALK · THE ENDHow I ended

At the end, I told them I’d send a PDF with all the online learning platforms and courses I know, so they could explore them. It would also include the courses I’ve done myself, in case they wanted to know.

Then I thanked Mahamuni Sir for giving me the chance to speak to them, genuinely, from the heart. And I told the students: if you have any questions, ask without any hesitation.

LOOKING BACKWhy this one was different

I’ve done speeches, seminar presentations and project presentations before. A guidance talk was new for me.

In a seminar or project presentation, you show your work and someone judges it. In a guidance talk, nobody is checking your slides. You’re sharing your own experience and trusting people with it, and what you say could change how someone spends their next three years.

That’s a different kind of responsibility, and a different kind of nervous. 🙂

LOOKING BACK“Invited while still in college” 🎇

I listen to many people online. A common story goes like this: they finish college, become successful in life, and then get invited back to give a talk at their own college.

Mine happened earlier. I was invited while I was still in college, in my final year. And honestly, I feel really proud of that. 🎇

I don’t say this to brag. It showed me that you don’t need to wait until you’re “successful” for your experience to be useful. It’s useful the moment you’ve learned something that someone else is still trying to learn.

THE FEARThe fear of public speaking 😬

Like many people, I fear public speaking. The fast heartbeat, the dry mouth, the feeling that everyone is judging you. I get all of it.

And the only thing that has ever really worked is this: the only way to overcome the fear is to face it.

Not reading about it, not planning for it, not waiting until you feel ready. Facing it, one real room at a time. The fear didn’t disappear when I walked in. It just got smaller than my willingness to do it.

TAKEAWAYSWhat I took from it 📌

  1. Share your journey, even when it feels small. This invitation came because students found my LinkedIn profile. I never posted to get invited. I shared what I was learning, and someone found it useful.
  2. Let preparation give your nerves somewhere to go. The script didn’t remove my nervousness, but it gave it a direction.
  3. Say yes to the room that scares you. Every time I’ve faced a fear, it was smaller than the version in my head.
Takeaway

If there’s a talk, a presentation or a room that scares you, I hope this nudges you towards it. Be nervous. Do it anyway. 👍

I hope you gained something from it.

— Yash
