Six Semesters, Six Different Me’s

How my perspective changed every semester of diploma days, and why I’m glad it did.

I was reflecting on the semesters I’ve passed in my diploma college days when I noticed something odd: I never actually decided to change how I see things. It just kept happening, quietly, in the background, like a phone updating overnight. Every semester arrived with its own goals, its own priorities and its own mindset, and looking back, each one was a small upgrade over the one before it.

I didn’t notice any of it while it was happening. Only when I laid all six semesters side by side did the pattern show up. So I’m writing it down while it’s still fresh. Think of this as a travel diary, except the place I travelled through was my own head.

SEM 1–2Everything was new

In the first two semesters I had no goal and no clarity about what I was supposed to be doing. Everything around me was new: the campus, the people, the rhythm of lectures, the odd freedom of nobody checking your homework every day. I wasn’t chasing anything. I was exploring, taking things in as they came.

My perspective was small, and honestly, I think that’s what kept it pure. I went to lectures. I was properly into learning the subjects, not just surviving them. Alongside the syllabus I practised DSA and DP, and I watched YouTube videos on nearly every topic we studied. Not in the panic week before exams, but almost every day. Nobody told me to, and there was no reward waiting. I simply liked the feeling of a concept clicking into place.

no goals. just curiosity.

Then exams would come closer and the mood changed. The library and the quiet corners around campus filled up with a few classmates and me, and I found myself running small lectures for them, mostly on Physics and Chemistry. It was a study group on paper but a classroom without a teacher in practice. Explaining a topic out loud to someone else turned out to be the fastest way to find out whether I actually understood it.

I didn’t have a goal yet, but I had curiosity. Looking back, that was enough to start. — note to self

SEM 3The certificate I couldn’t explain

The first real shift in my perspective began with an embarrassing moment in the 2nd semester, one that took an entire vacation to digest.

THE MOMENT

During the viva for DLS (Development of Life Skills), our professor asked each of us to submit a certificate we had earned. I had nothing. I’d been learning from YouTube for months and had no certificate to show for any of it. So I handed in the only one I had: an appreciation certificate from my society’s internal gathering, given because I had cleared my 10th SSC. Which was, let’s be honest, pretty normal. Not exactly an achievement.

Sir looked at it and asked:

“What is the significance of this certificate?”

“What did you learn from it?”

I was blank. Not nervous-blank, but truly empty, because there was no honest answer to give.

That silence followed me out of the room. It wasn’t that I hadn’t learned anything. It was that I had nothing I could point to, nothing that said this is what I did and this is what it taught me. I realised that learning is only half the job. The other half is being able to show it.

So I made a decision. From then on I wouldn’t just go through courses, I would finish them and earn the certificate at the end. Over a few months of vacation I worked through certified courses, one of which was a DSA in Python course by Jovial ML.

It was a small change, from “I’m learning” to “I’m learning, and I can prove it.” But it was also the first time I started thinking about my future in a structured way, not as something that would just happen to me.

SEM 4Stepping outside my own campus

By the fourth semester, study, exams and submissions weren’t enough to fill my head. I wanted to see what else was out there. So I started participating in inter-college competitions, mostly coding contests and tech quizzes, while still keeping up with everything else college demanded.

I took part in many and won a few, and yes, the wins felt great. But what actually changed was what I was chasing. I wanted to explore as many colleges around us as I could, walk into their events, talk to new students and build connections with people I’d never have met otherwise. Every competition was really an excuse to meet a new campus.

That was the semester I noticed my world was bigger than my college. Until then, “college” meant one building and the same faces. Suddenly it meant a whole city of students, all working through their own version of the same journey.

SEM 4 · VACATIONThe internship we almost didn’t get

At the end of that vacation came vocational training, and with it a lesson that wasn’t in any syllabus. We didn’t want to wait for a company to be handed to us. So my friend Anand and I decided to find one ourselves, by physically going everywhere we could.

We covered the city and the industrial areas on a bike, on trips of 100+ km, marking every location on Google Maps as we went. At almost every stop the story ended in one of three ways: the place was closed, it didn’t offer internships, or it wanted our money.

That third one was the pattern. Several places were happy to give us an “internship certificate” plus some training, for a fee. It was what many of our seniors and classmates had done, and it was starting to look like the only way in. After enough locked doors, I was almost ready to quit.

Anand wouldn’t let me. He’s my best friend and my classmate, and he suggested one last try: go back to the Software IT Park area one more time. So the two of us rode there again.

And there it was: a startup called Modi Innovations Pvt Ltd, open at the very moment we walked up. We asked whether they could give us an internship, and we ended up talking directly to the head of the startup’s branch in the IT Park, Abhishek Sir.

He said yes. We told him how much effort we’d put into the search, and how many places had offered us paid training instead. We were honest that most of our seniors and classmates had taken that route and that we’d been about to take it as a last option too. That’s when Abhishek Sir taught us something I still carry with me:

An internship is learning while you work. If someone asks you to pay for doing their work, say no. If I ever ask you to pay, leave here too. — Abhishek Sir, as I remember it

He also cleared up a misconception that scams quietly depend on. Some internships are paid, which means the company pays you. A lot of students hear “paid internship” and assume it means they have to pay, and that confusion is exactly what lets fake programs charge fees.

paid internship = they pay you.

We had been about to take the paid option as a last resort. Instead we left with a real internship, and a lesson that saved us from paying for something that should have been paying us.

SEM 5From participating to organising

If the fourth semester was about participating, the fifth was about organising. I was selected as the Event Manager of the department, which meant I stopped only showing up to events and started running them. I managed departmental events and helped build a magazine and a website for the committee.

It’s a very different feeling to be the person an event depends on. You see the work behind the curtain, the parts nobody claps for, and you start to appreciate every event you ever attended as a spectator.

My study approach changed too. I became more strategic and more serious about scoring well, with the intention of getting the best results I could, and I wanted to be seen by my teachers as someone who gave everything. I was doing everything possible: for the exams, for the events, for the impression I was leaving.

SEM 5 · PEOPLEThe friendship I chose on purpose

This semester also held one of the most memorable and thoughtful decisions of my life.

Everyone knows friendship isn’t something you can force. It builds by itself with the people you match vibes with, share interests with and spend time around. But in the fifth semester, for the first time in my life, I picked a friendship on purpose. I looked at who I’d genuinely want to learn from, and then I tried to build the bond.

His name was Shubham, a student from the Computer Engineering department, and the best student in his class. I was inspired by him, truly. So I started following his orbit: talking to him, sharing what I knew. And it turned out he was fascinated by my programming skills and technical knowledge, which gave us something to trade.

From him I learned how to prepare for exams, study better, score better and get noticed by teachers. He learned how to study collaboratively instead of competitively, along with programming fundamentals like HTML, Android and more. Shubham is still my best friend today.

I didn’t abandon my old friends, not for a moment. I had simply started spending more of my time with Shubham and his roommates, people who took studying seriously, so my days drifted toward the Computer Engineering crowd and, for a while, away from my AIML classmates. It was a nice experience of drifting, one I chose with my eyes open.

SEM 6No space for regrets

The final semester was full of everything: activities, events, work, projects, laughs, mistakes, responsibilities, emotions, memories. Honestly it was more than I could comfortably carry, and still I went through almost all of it.

My perspective this time was simple. This was the last semester, so I wanted to spend it to the fullest, with as many experiences as I could fit in, so there would be no room for “I wish I had” later. Sports, gatherings, events, final-year projects, and whatnot. I said yes to almost everything, and “almost” is where the best stories live.

Not everything went perfectly, which is exactly why I remember it. A few moments from that semester:

  • The carrom win I missed on stage I was part of the carrom team, and we won. But when the winners were called up, the speaker didn’t pronounce my name correctly, and in the confusion I missed my moment: walking up, taking the prize and shaking hands with the principal, the trustees and everyone else on stage. We won, and I still laugh about the one part I didn’t get to do.
  • What carrom cost me The matches were scheduled on the same day as our cultural events, so I couldn’t attend many of the great things happening on campus. And then, to make it a little sadder, our teacher didn’t allow us to play the further interzonal matches, because our internal exams fell on the same day.
  • The pani puri stall The day before the carrom matches was our traditional day, along with a funfair at college. My best friend Mayur had a pani puri stall, and as I was passing by, he pulled me in to help serve plates to a crowd that simply didn’t end. His stall won first prize, and I was genuinely happy for him. The catch: I’d spent the day behind the counter instead of in the photos, so I missed taking pictures with our classmates. He missed them too.
  • The stage act I was also managing the art and design team for our department’s stage act, themed on Jammu & Kashmir. A few girls from the DDGM department worked under me to get everything done. Another responsibility, and another thing I’d never have done if I hadn’t said yes.

None of those memories are perfect, and I wouldn’t trade a single imperfect one. I didn’t leave the last semester with a list of things I should have done. I left with a list of things I did, mispronounced name and missing photos included.

LOOKING BACKWhat I took from it

Put all six semesters in one place and the pattern is hard to miss. This is what my notebook margin would look like: 

sem 1–2  →  curiosity
sem 3    →  proof
sem 4    →  connections
sem 5    →  leadership
sem 6    →  fullness   # no-regrets mode
  • Curiosity came first, then proof, then people, then leadership, then fullness.
  • None of the shifts were planned. I only saw the pattern when I looked back.
  • Each version of me was right for its semester, and each one made the next version possible.
Takeaway

I’m sharing this because I feel many of you go through such mental shifts in your own journey. Your beliefs and your thinking about certain things change during these years, and it’s great to see it happen, to watch your mindset take shape along the way. If you’re in the middle of a semester right now: which version of you is it?

Hope it was insightful.

— Yash
